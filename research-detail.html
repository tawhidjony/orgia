The Art of Jamdani Weaving

Dr. Ayesha Begum March 15, 2026 8 min read

In the narrow lanes of Rupganj, Dhaka, the rhythmic clatter of wooden looms has echoed for over five centuries. Here, jamdani — the finest muslin weave in the world — continues to be crafted by hand, one supplementary weft thread at a time.

Jamdani is not merely a fabric; it is a testament to human patience and artistry. Recognized by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, this supplementary weft technique creates intricate floral and geometric patterns that appear to float on the sheer muslin ground.

A History Woven in Thread

The origins of jamdani trace back to the Mughal era, when Dhaka muslin was prized across the courts of Asia and Europe. Portuguese, Dutch, and British traders marveled at its gossamer lightness — a single saree could pass through a ring. The jamdani technique elevated this already extraordinary fabric with patterns of unparalleled delicacy.

During the British colonial period, the muslin industry suffered devastating decline as machine-made textiles flooded the market. Yet jamdani weaving persisted in pockets of Dhaka, passed down through generations of weaver families who refused to let the tradition die.

The Weaving Process

Creating a jamdani saree is a labor of extraordinary dedication. A master weaver, working alongside two assistants, may spend two to three months on a single piece. The process begins with preparing the fine cotton yarn, which is spun, dyed with natural colors, and wound onto bobbins.

At the loom, the weaver works the ground weave while assistants insert each supplementary weft thread individually using a bamboo splinter called a "kantha." No mechanical aid is involved — every motif is placed by hand, making each jamdani saree truly unique.

Revival in Contemporary Fashion

Today, jamdani is experiencing a remarkable renaissance. Bangladeshi designers like Rima Sultana and Sabrina Chowdhury are incorporating jamdani into contemporary silhouettes — fusion gowns, structured blazers, and avant-garde evening wear — bringing this ancient craft to global fashion weeks and luxury boutiques.

Organizations like the Bengal Foundation and Aarong have invested in weaver cooperatives, ensuring fair wages and sustainable production. The result is a thriving ecosystem where tradition and innovation coexist, securing jamdani's place in the fashion landscape for generations to come.

"Each thread in a jamdani saree carries the soul of Bengal. When you wear jamdani, you wear history."

As we look to the future, the challenge remains: how to scale jamdani production without compromising its handmade integrity. Digital platforms like ORGIA.BD play a crucial role in connecting weavers directly with designers and consumers worldwide, ensuring that this irreplaceable art form continues to flourish.
